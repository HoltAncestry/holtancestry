Jacobite Rising 1745

The Jacobite rising of 1745 was an attempt by Charles Edward Stuart to regain the British throne for his father, James Francis Edward Stuart.

The Holts and the Jacobite Rising of 1745

This account draws on the nineteenth‑century local histories of William Robertson, including Old and New Rochdale (1881) and Rochdale Past and Present (1876), whose chronicles preserve the oral traditions and written records surrounding Valentine Holt’s fate during the Jacobite rebellion.

The Forty‑Five: A Shadow Over Lancashire

The year 1745 marked one of the most dramatic moments in British history. As Charles Edward Stuart’s army advanced south, the tension in London was mirrored by the divided loyalties of the northern counties. In Rochdale, the Jacobite presence was not merely a distant political disturbance but a local crisis that forced long‑established families to choose between the Hanoverian government and the romantic, perilous hope of restoring the Stuart line.

Valentine Holt: The Stubley Heir and the Rebel Cause

Among those who cast their lot with the Jacobite cause was Valentine Holt, a descendant of the Holts of Stubley—an ancient family rooted for centuries in Littleborough and Rochdale. Robertson describes Valentine as a man of restless temperament, ill‑suited to the quiet life of a rural squire. When the Young Pretender reached Manchester in late November 1745, the atmosphere was electric. Although Rochdale itself was not occupied by the main Jacobite force, its influence was unmistakable.

Valentine Holt travelled to Manchester to volunteer for the “Manchester Regiment,” the only English regiment raised in support of Charles Edward Stuart.

From Volunteer to Martyr: The End at Carlisle

The Jacobite advance faltered after the council at Derby, and the army retreated northward. A small garrison—including members of the Manchester Regiment—was left to defend Carlisle. As the Duke of Cumberland’s artillery pounded the walls of Carlisle Castle, the defenders faced a stark choice: surrender or death.

According to Robertson’s accounts, Valentine Holt was among those who met their end during the siege. He was shot at Carlisle, far from the familiar landscape of Stubley Hall. His death stands as a solemn reminder of the personal cost of the Forty‑Five—a rebellion that promised a kingdom but delivered only loss for many families of Lancashire.
